**Research Program III: Convergence of Chemical Biology Technologies**

One of the most challenging problems in the area of biomedical research is the development of tools/technologies that are highly specific to follow particular biological processes in a spatio-temporal manner in living animals. Over the past 10 years, we have successfully developed specific imaging methods that can be used to monitor the function of active protein in live cells in real-time with high spatiotemporal resolution. As most of the advanced chemical probes will not work in in vivo conditions hence development of novel delivery systems is imperative for targeted delivery of chemical probes. Although several scaffolds are currently used for this purpose, all of them have some kind of limitations. In this regard, we invented a new method MAPLabTech. This technology was utilized to synthesize libraries of self-assembling artificial proteins with very structural diversity. These custom-designed proteins spontaneously form well-defined protein nanoparticles.

Although we have made substantial progress in both research programs there are many pending challenges as outlined below:

- Encapsulation of fluorescent chemical probe (FCP) inside the protein nanoparticles (PN)
- Stability and in vitro leakage studies of PN
- Demonstration of ABRGT at the cellular level using protein nanoparticles containing FCP
- Toxicity studies of protein nanoparticles at the cellular level
- Creation of a new tumor cell line expressing caspase-3/RLuc fusion protein utilizing CRISPR/PRIME editing technologies
- Biodistribution studies of PN
- Blood and tissue PK of PN
- Dose escalation/toxicity studies of PN
- Finally, ABRGT and MAPLabTech will be integrated and will be used to image the function of "active proteins" in living animals in real time and the same technology will be used for in vivo drug screening of anti-cancer drugs (Figure 11)

In the future, we will translate this technology to decipher path (physiological) processes in humans. We are aware that the current optical methods developed in our lab may not be suited for in vivo imaging of humans but the concept of this technology can be integrated into other imaging modalities such as PET/CT/MRI for whole-body molecular imaging of humans.
