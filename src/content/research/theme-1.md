***RP-I: Development of Robust Specific Imaging Method***

The ability to watch various biological processes in real-time with a very high spatial resolution has changed the understanding of simple biochemical reactions in the context of systems biology. Therefore, the development of a powerful imaging method is very important to achieve this goal. This field is broadly classified into three core areas:

(i) Design and development of novel imaging probes/methods

(ii) Development of the most sophisticated instruments

(iii) Image data analysis

The past two decades have witnessed tremendous progress in the area of instrumentation engineering and applied physics that led to the invention of high-end imaging instruments with very high capability such as super-resolution and intra-vital microscopes. Similarly, the field of image analyses has seen exponential growth because of the implementation of machine learning and artificial intelligence. Considering this tremendous progress in recent years, one would anticipate, these technologies would have helped to decipher various biological processes that were not able to accomplish before. However, we still lack robust, simple, powerful, and highly specific imaging methods. This is mostly attributed to incremental progress in the area of probe design/method development.

***Development of Optical Method for an Accurate Detection of Enzymatic Activity In Vivo***

Enzymes play a very important role in various biological processes and therefore enzyme activity is tightly regulated at multiple levels. Most of the enzymes are present in their inactive forms such as zymogen or bound by endogenous inhibitors. Besides, enzymes are subjected to ubiquitination followed by degradation in the proteasomal compartment. In essence, the concentration of active enzyme is tightly regulated and any alteration of active enzyme concentration would lead to pathological conditions such as cancer, arthritis, and autoimmune diseases. Therefore, developing optical imaging methods for exquisite imaging of target "active enzyme" is highly desirable. During the past three decades, there have been lots of efforts in developing novel molecular probes or imaging methods for the specific detection of active enzymes. Most of the existing probes are specific to particular enzyme families; they were not able to distinguish different enzymes in the same family thus producing an ambiguous signal. This long-standing problem was addressed by the invention of activity-based fluorescent probes (ABFPs). ABFPs covalently label enzyme active site and report the concentration of "active enzyme". Although selectivity is still a major concern in this method, it does provide opportunities to capture the labeling of different proteins by post-processing of tissue or cell lysate after imaging experiment by doing in-gel fluorescence studies or mass spec measurements. However, these studies require tissue homogenization, and therefore spatial information is lost. More importantly, these methods cannot be applied to proteins, which are expressed in very low concentrations and for single-cell analysis.

## Past Research

***Activity-based Reporter Gene Technology (AbRGT)***

***AbRGT-FRET Method***

To address the shortcoming of the previous imaging methods, our group invented a new method called "**A**ctivity-**B**ased **R**eporter **G**ene **T**echnology (ABRGT)". For the first time, we have shown that the activity of the target enzyme can be detected with an absolute specificity with high spatial-temporal resolution in a living cell using a FRET-based readout (Figure 3). The most attractive feature of this technology is that it is an extremely simple method. Since this is a platform technology, we have shown that this method could be used to image multiple enzymes, independently. We have also demonstrated that our method could be successfully used for inhibitor screening in live or intact cells (Figure 3b).

ABRGT-FRET offers several advantages such as (i) a simple and powerful method, (ii) offers protein profiling in live cells with unprecedented specificity, (iii) real time imaging capabilities, (iv) longitudinal imaging, and (v) in principle could be adopted to study the function of any protein (platform technology). Although extremely powerful, like any other method, this technology also has a few limitations such as (i) the need for highly sophisticated instruments such as a confocal microscope, (ii) narrow dynamic range, (iii) low-throughput, (iv) the need for highly trained personnel and (v) most importantly this technology may not be translated to protein profiling in live animal as FRET study may not be ideal for whole-body imaging experiments.

***ABRGT-BRET Method***

To address the limitation of the ABRGT-FRET method, recently we have introduced another platform technology, named ABPP-BRET. We demonstrate the capability of this method to detect post-translational modification of the caspase-3 enzyme in live cells in real time in a longitudinal way. This method was used for real-time target engagement/inhibitor profiling in live cells (Figure 4). Most importantly, this technology provides opportunities to translate ABRGT-BRET to study protein function with an unprecedented specificity in living animals in real time.

## Present Work

***Research Program I***

In the past decade, we have demonstrated that ABRGT-FRET and ABRGT-BRET technologies could be successfully used to decipher the function of various active cysteine proteases such as caspase-3, -7, -8, -9, and cathepsin-B in living cells in real-time in a non-invasive manner. Building on that, we are currently working on applying these technologies to understand the functions of other enzyme families such as serine hydrolases (SH). SH is one of the largest enzyme families in the mammalian system. They are implicated in various pathological conditions such as cancer, arthritis, etc. Benjamin Cravatt and others applied activity-based protein profiling (ABPP) coupled with the mass spectrometry method to elucidate the function of many serine hydrolases. However, there are other serine proteases whose function still needs to be studied in detail. Htra2 enzyme is one such protein and it is involved in cancer. Although, there are few reports on the role of htra2 in cancer a complete understanding of htra2 is still lacking. We are currently involved in studying the function of the htra2 protein utilizing ABRGT-FRET technology. Preliminary studies suggest that htra2-GFP fusion could be expressed in living HeLa cells as evident (Figure 6). In parallel, we are involved in the synthesis of fluorescent activity-based probes containing fluorophosphonate ward head and rhodamine dye (which can act as an acceptor for GFP). Imaging studies are under-way and the results will be communicated soon.
