***Research Program - II***

The field of biomolecular nanotechnologies has contributed significantly to the design of molecular scaffolds that are based on DNA, RNA, peptides, and carbohydrates. Among the various biomolecular nanotechnologies, protein nanotechnology is relatively new and highly challenging to practice. However, the rapid advances in synthetic gene synthesis and the highly predictive power of computational protein design have led to the design of elaborate protein structures with high accuracy. Although useful, this method often yields protein assemblies, which are static. This is in sharp contrast to natural protein assemblies, which are known to exhibit highly dynamic behavior achieved through various post-translational modifications. In addition, the computational protein design method is mostly restricted to standard 20 amino acids with very few exceptions. On the other hand, chemical strategies provide enormous opportunities to design protein super-structures that can exhibit dynamic behavior. However, most of the chemical methods are not considered mature technologies because these methods often yield a polydisperse sample that lacks high-resolution structural information, and more importantly, these methods are restricted to a few proteins.

***Directed Chemical Evolution of Self-Assembling Artificial Proteins***

To address these challenges, our group initiated a research program "Directed Chemical Evolution of Self-Assembling Artificial Proteins". We are interested in designing facially amphiphilic self-assembling artificial proteins containing two distinct domains i.e. hydrophilic globular protein connected to the hydrophobic domain through a linear flexible linker. More importantly, we are interested in creating a platform technology to design diverse artificial proteins (SAPs) from a wide variety of globular proteins (Figure 5A). Although the molecular design looks simple, it is extremely difficult to synthesize these proteins because of the following reasons; (i) the first major bottleneck is the orthogonal solubility properties of a hydrophilic globular protein and hydrophobic synthetic molecule, and (ii) purification is highly challenging, as it resembles membrane proteins. To address these issues, we invented a new method called "**M**icelle-**A**ssisted **P**rotein **Lab**eling **Tech**nology (**MAPLabTech**)". Over the last decade, we used this technology to make a diverse set of stimuli-responsive self-assembling artificial proteins from a wide variety of proteins (Figure 5B). We have demonstrated that the size, surface charge, molecular mass, and oligomeric state of synthetic protein assemblies can be systematically tuned through molecular design. We have used this technology to make well-defined monodisperse protein amphiphiles, protein-dendron conjugates, photo-responsive protein amphiphiles, protein-peptide amphiphiles, photo-responsive protein-dendron conjugates, redox-responsive protein-dendron conjugates. Although **MAPLabTech 1.0** provides an opportunity to work with a wide range of proteins, it is still limited to hundreds of proteins. To expand the utility of this method, we designed a new chemical probe containing "N-pyridyl-carboxaldehyde". This chemical probe can react with any globular protein containing free N-terminal amine functionality thus covering vast protein space. This methodology (**MAPLabTech 2.0**) was used to make protein amphiphiles from various protein families irrespective of their structural and functional classification. Finally, we also designed an amphiphilic chemical probe containing a maleimide functionality and this probe was used to make protein amphiphiles from proteins containing a single surface-exposed cysteine residue (**MAPLabTech 3.0**). Although MAPLabTech is extremely powerful, it still involves a lot of chemical synthesis to make SAPs and there to make this method more attractive, we introduced a new method that involves copper-free click chemistry for expedited synthesis of SAPs.

## Present Work

***Research Program II***

Micelle-assisted protein labeling technology (MAPLabTech) has emerged as a mature technology for the synthesis of well-defined monodisperse self-assembling artificial proteins. Although extremely powerful, like any other method, this technology also has a few limitations such as incomplete reaction, low yield, and pretty laborious purifications. To address some of the limitations of MAPLabTech, we are currently working on two different technologies.

***Synthesis of SAPs Utilizing Catalytic Host-Guest System***

In this work, we used cyclodextrin (CD) as a host to solubilize the highly hydrophobic chemical probes (Figure 7). The solubility results indicated that CD could solubilize many probes irrespective of their hydrophobicity. As a test reaction, we conducted protein bioconjugation of N-terminal probes in the presence of γ-cyclodextrin. To our surprise, we saw successful bioconjugation as evident from MALDI-ToF results (Figure 8A). Although this is a nice results, it does not offer any advantage compared to our previous technology (MAPLabTech). We suspect the slow reaction rate of these chemical probes could be the reason for the poor yield. In addition, the presence of unreacted protein should be removed and hence involve the extra purification step and hence limit the utility of this method. Previous work from other laboratories and our lab demonstrated the fluorophosphonate chemical probe reacts instantaneously with the serine residue of serine proteases. If that is true, the γ-cyclodextrin should be able to solubilize the fluorophosphonate chemical probe and also should facilitate quantitative site-specific bioconjugation (Figure 8B). Hence we tested the bioconjugation of the fluoro-phosphonate probe in the presence of cyclodextrin. To our delight, we observed near quantitative yield as evident from MALDI-MS results.

***Synthesis of SAPs Utilizing Solubility Tags***

In our previous work, we used non-covalent chemistry (micelle and cyclodextrin) to solubilize the hydrophobic chemical probes and thereby achieved successful bioconjugation (Figure 9). Although extremely useful, it is better to develop alternate technologies so that the community would have a suite of technologies available. Accordingly we designed a macomolecular probe. the chemical probe contains five structural elements: (i) protein reactive warhead functionality (ii) hydrophilic linker (iii) hydrophobic tail (iv) cleavable functionality, and (v) solubilizing tag (Figure 10). We hypothesize that the presence of a solubility tag would make the chemical probe soluble in an aqueous medium and thereby facilitate site-specific bioconjugation reaction and yield highly hydrophilic non-self-assembling monomeric protein. After bioconjugation, the solubility tag will be selectively removed to afford facially amphiphilic proteins and thus facilitate self-assembly of protein bioconjugate into protein nanoassemblies of defined size.

***Structural Biology Studies of Protein Nanoassemblies***

Computational protein design/genetic method emerges as a mature technology for the design of artificial protein assemblies. To date, this method is used to make hundreds of artificial protein assemblies, and their structures were determined to near-atomic level using cryo-TEM. On the other hand, MAPLabTech is also emerging as a powerful technology to design highly monodisperse protein nanoassemblies of defined size. SEC-MALS (Figure 10A) and SANS studies revealed these assemblies have defined shapes and precise oligomeric states. Encouraged by these biophysical results, we have initiated structural biology studies of protein nanoassemblies in collaboration with another laboratory. The 2-D classification results from cryo-TEM are very encouraging (Figure 10B). Future research will be directed to solving the structure of macromolecular complexes at near-atomic resolution.
